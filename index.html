I.

Start here: the ideas in plain English

You do not need any background in organisational change to use this guide. This opening section explains, in plain language, what the guide is for, the handful of ideas everything else rests on, and how to find your way around.

What this guide is for

Lending Well described what a risk-led lending culture looks like: people rewarded for the long-term quality of the loans they make, credit colleagues involved early rather than at the last minute, concerns raised while they are still cheap to fix, and leaders who celebrate a well-reasoned “no” as much as a big completion. Most lenders agree with all of that. The hard part is getting there. Culture cannot be ordered into existence by a memo, and training courses on their own rarely change what people do on a Thursday afternoon when a deal has to close by Friday.

This guide is about the getting there. It sets out a complete process — who does what, in what order, with which tools, and how you will know it is working — for changing a lending culture so that it becomes a genuine competitive advantage rather than a compliance exercise.

Why culture is a credit risk

Every loan passes through many hands: the relationship manager who brings it in, the analyst who prepares the paper, the valuer, the credit officer, the committee, and — if things go wrong — the recoveries team. Each of them makes small judgements: whether a comparable is too old, whether a cash-flow assumption is too rosy, whether to mention a doubt in committee. Policy shapes some of those judgements, but most are shaped by what people believe will happen to them if they speak up, slow a deal down or say no. That belief is the culture. When it points the wrong way, risk accumulates quietly in the book and surfaces years later as losses. When it points the right way, the same people catch problems early, structure deals better, and give good borrowers confident answers faster.

Why this is hard — and why most attempts fail

Three things make lending culture stubborn. First, delays: the reward for loosening standards arrives this year, but the loss arrives after the bonus has been paid, so the people who caused it rarely feel it. Second, incentives: if pay and promotion reward volume, no amount of talk about prudence will compete. Third, silence: people often know about problems but stay quiet because raising them feels risky or pointless. A programme that only communicates values leaves all three untouched. This guide works on all three directly.

II.

Culture is the part of the credit process you cannot see on the file

Every lender has a credit culture, whether written down or not. It is the sum of what people actually reward, tolerate, notice and say out loud when a deal is on the table — and it decides whether the policy manual is read or merely filed.

Lending Well set out what a risk-led lending culture looks like: incentives tied to risk-adjusted, multi-year outcomes; credit involved at the term sheet rather than the final committee; risk appetite translated into thresholds the front line can use; psychological safety to surface deteriorating loans early; cross-functional training; shared deal forums; dynamic stress testing; early warning; a worst-practice library; and leadership that rewards a disciplined “no” as visibly as a major completion.

This guide is the how. It treats culture change not as a communications campaign but as the redesign of the conditions in which lending decisions are made — the incentives, structures, conversations, rituals and measures that produce behaviour. It draws on the thinkers who built the field of organisational change, from Lewin's action research and the Tavistock coal-mine studies to dialogic, paradox and complexity approaches, and tests each idea against what is now known about how loan officers, credit teams and lending institutions actually behave.

VI.

The architecture: seven movements

The process is sequenced but not linear. Each movement has a purpose, a set of thinkers who inform it, concrete interventions, outputs and a gate that must be passed before scaling. Movements overlap: diagnosis never really stops, and learning in action feeds back into redesign. The design deliberately combines episodic change — a few big, structural redesigns such as incentives and committee architecture — with continuous change in everyday routines, because lending cultures need both.

Readiness: will this change move?

The change formula

Change moves when dissatisfaction with the present, a compelling vision and credible first steps together outweigh the cost and resistance of changing. Because the three are multiplied, if any one is near zero the whole product collapses. Set the levels you observe for one stakeholder group.

The five beliefs scan

People commit when they believe five things: there is a real gap (discrepancy); this change is the right answer (appropriateness); we can do it (efficacy); leaders really mean it (principal support); and it is good for me (valence). Score each group from 1 (not believed) to 5 (firmly believed).

X.

Force-field analysis: what holds the culture in place?

Lewin's insight was that behaviour sits where driving and restraining forces balance. Pushing harder on the drivers tends to raise tension and resistance; weakening the restrainers lets behaviour move with less effort. Set the strength of each force as you see it, add your own, and read where to act.

XI.

People, emotion and resistance

Resistance is information, not insubordination. Coch and French's founding insight was that resistance comes from the context of a change rather than from the individual, and that participation in designing it reduces it. Later work shows that some people are dispositionally more change-averse — through preference for routine, emotional reaction to imposed change, short-term focus and cognitive rigidity — but the practical lesson is design, not diagnosis of individuals. Middle managers carry the emotional load: those who both champion the change and attend to their teams' feelings produce adaptation; those who do only one produce inertia or chaos.

XII.

The hidden costs of the current culture

A culture does not show up as a line in the accounts — its dysfunctions do, scattered and unnamed: re-trades, rework when credit arrives late, deals lost to slow certainty, avoidable defaults, workout costs, recovery shortfalls and the turnover of good people. The socio-economic approach to management makes these hidden costs visible, so that culture change can be argued as an investment with a return rather than a cost of compliance.

XIII.

The credit cycle as a system

Lending cultures oscillate because of feedback loops with delays in them. Volume targets loosen structuring and reward growth; losses surface years later; clampdowns cut good business with bad; pressure for volume returns. The programme is designed to weaken the vicious loops and strengthen the virtuous one. Click a loop or a node.

XIV.

Paradoxes to hold, not problems to solve

Lending well means living with tensions that never go away: growth and discipline, speed and scrutiny, partnership and enforcement, rules and judgement. Treating them as either/or choices produces cycles of over-correction — a loosening phase, losses, a clampdown, lost business, a loosening phase. A both/and approach accepts the tension, separates and connects the poles, and moves deliberately between them. Move each slider to where your organisation currently leans.

XV.

Which motor is driving your change?

Change in organisations is produced by four different motors: a prescribed sequence (life-cycle), a purposeful goal (teleological), conflict between groups (dialectical), and selection by the environment (evolutionary). Most lending culture programmes run on more than one at once — a regulatory programme, a strategy, a running tension between origination and credit, and a market that rewards some lenders and eliminates others. Knowing which motor is running tells you how the change will break down and what to do about it.

XIX.

The regulatory frame (UK, November 2026)

Regulation does not create a good lending culture, but it sets the floor and increasingly asks firms to evidence culture. The timeline below is current as at November 2026. Non-bank lenders operate under different regimes, but the supervisory logic — purpose, leadership, people management and governance as the drivers of behaviour — applies to every lender.

The FSB's four indicators of a sound risk culture

  • Tone from the top — leaders' behaviour, not only statements.
  • Accountability — people know and own the risks they take.
  • Effective communication and challenge — dissent is possible and acted on.
  • Incentives — pay and progression reward the right risk behaviour.

The movements in §VI are mapped to these four in the measurement architecture (§XVIII).

The FCA's four drivers of culture

  • Purpose — clearly communicated and linked to roles.
  • Leadership — including middle managers, who carry culture day to day.
  • Approach to rewarding and managing people.
  • Governance, systems and controls.
